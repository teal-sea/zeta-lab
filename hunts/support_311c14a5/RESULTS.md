## 1. Setup and notation

$S = \{p_1,\dots,p_k\}$ distinct primes. $A \subset \mathbb{Z}_{>0}$ finite with
distinct elements, admissible if every off-diagonal sum $a+b$ ($a \ne b$) has no
prime factor outside $S$. $g(k) = \max |A|$ over all such $S$ and $A$.
An **$S$-unit** is a rational whose numerator and denominator are $S$-smooth;
they form a group $\Gamma_S \le \mathbb{Q}^*$ of rank $k$ (torsion $\{\pm 1\}$).
Admissibility says exactly: all off-diagonal sums are positive $S$-units.

Erdős #126 is $f(n)/\log n \to \infty$, equivalently (parent §1, checked and
correct) $\log g(k) = o(k)$. Erdős–Turán 1934: $g(k) < 3 \cdot 2^{k-1}$.

## 2. The reduction. **Proved.**

> **Proposition.** Let $A$ be admissible for $S$, $|A| = n \ge 3$. Fix any two
> distinct $a, b \in A$ and put $d = a - b \ne 0$, $\alpha = 1/d$,
> $\beta = -1/d$. Then the map
> $$c \;\longmapsto\; (X, Y) = (a+c,\; b+c)$$
> sends $A \setminus \{a,b\}$ **injectively** into the solution set of
> $$\alpha X + \beta Y = 1, \qquad X, Y \in \Gamma_S .$$
> Consequently $n - 2 \le E(k)$, where
> $$E(k) \;:=\; \max_{S,\;|S|=k}\;\; \max_{\alpha,\beta \in \mathbb{Q}^*}\;
> \#\{(X,Y) \in \Gamma_S^2 : \alpha X + \beta Y = 1\},$$
> so $\;g(k) \le 2 + E(k)$.

*Proof.* For $c \in A \setminus \{a,b\}$ both $a+c$ and $b+c$ are off-diagonal
sums, hence positive $S$-units. And $(a+c) - (b+c) = a - b = d$, so dividing by
$d$ gives $\alpha(a+c) + \beta(b+c) = 1$. Injectivity: $X = a+c$ determines $c$.
$\square$

Three things are worth saying about how cheap that is.

**No rank inflation.** The naive move, dividing the difference $u - v = d$ by $d$
and absorbing $1/d$ into the group, costs a generator and lands in rank $k+1$.
Putting the constants in the *coefficients* instead of the group keeps the rank
at exactly $k$, and Evertse's theorem is stated for $\alpha X + \beta Y = 1$ with
arbitrary fixed nonzero rationals, so nothing is lost by doing so. (For the
asymptotic statement $k$ versus $k+1$ would not have mattered; for the constants
in §3 it does.)

**Uniformity in $\alpha, \beta$ is load-bearing and must be stated.** $d = a-b$
is not under our control and is not an $S$-unit in general. A bound on the number
of solutions that degraded with the height of $\alpha,\beta$ would be useless
here. Evertse's bound is uniform in $\alpha,\beta$; any replacement must be too.

**The three-term route is strictly worse.** Using four elements
$a,b,c,d$ and $(a+b)+(c+d) = (a+c)+(b+d)$ gives a three-term unit equation
$x+y+z=1$, nondegenerate for distinct elements (no vanishing subsum: two of the
three are positive, and each two-term subsum vanishing forces two elements of
$A$ to coincide). But the map from quadruples to solution triples has no
evident injectivity, and normalising the constant term costs the same extra
generator anyway. Two-term with an honest injection beats three-term with a
fibre problem.

**Verified on data.** `probe.py` §B re-derives the reduction on all seven of the
parent's witnesses ($k=1..7$): the identity $\alpha X + \beta Y = 1$ and the
$S$-unit property hold for all $\binom{n}{2}$ choices of $(a,b)$ on every
witness, and $c \mapsto X$ is injective in all 165 cases tested, with exactly
$n-2$ solutions produced each time. That is a check of the proof's mechanics,
not of the bound.

## 3. Quantitative dependence on $k$: what the route is worth today

| | bound on $E(k)$ | what it gives for $g(k)$ |
|---|---|---|
| Evertse 1984 (upper, $ax+by=1$ in $S$-units, $s = k+1$ places over $\mathbb{Q}$) | $\le e^{4s+6} = e^{4k+10}$ | $g(k) \le 2 + e^{4k+10}$ |
| Beukers–Schlickewei 1996 (upper, $x+y=1$, rank $r$) | $\le 2^{8(r+2)}$ | $g(k) \le 2 + 2^{8(k+3)}$ via the rank-$(k+1)$ variant |
| Erdős–Stewart–Tijdeman 1988 (**lower**, $ax+by=1$) | $\ge \exp\big((4+o(1))\sqrt{s/\log s}\big)$ | n/a |
| Erdős–Turán 1934 (elementary, direct) | n/a | $g(k) < 3\cdot 2^{k-1}$ |

*Annotated on landing (2026-10-21), not corrected.* The Evertse row's
$e^{4s+6}$ came from a web search, and the run's `HANDBACK.json` says not to
quote the exponents as pinned. Győry–Stewart–Tijdeman 1986 quote Evertse's
bound as $3\cdot7^{2w+3}$, read in their primary text by the sibling literature
arm (Hunt #143, `support_fcffa3b9` T4), and Hunts #96 and #106 use the same
$3\cdot7^{d+2s}$. With $s=k+1$ that is $3\cdot7^{2k+3} \approx e^{3.89k+6.94}$,
smaller than $e^{4k+10}$ for every $k \ge 0$, so on that reading the route's
base is $49$ rather than $e^4 \approx 54.6$. Either way it is far above $2$, and
fact (a) below is unchanged. The Beukers–Schlickewei row's $2^{8(r+2)}$ is
likewise from a search; main quotes $2^{8r+8}$ (Hunt #104; Hunt #143's T6,
secondary), and neither form was checked against the paper here.

Read the table honestly and two facts fall out, and they are the substance of
this arm.

**(a) The unit-equation route is currently much weaker than 1934.** Base
$e^4 \approx 54.6$ against base $2$. Any write-up that reaches for
"$S$-unit equations have finitely many solutions" as if it were progress on
#126 is citing qualitative finiteness where the quantity is the whole problem.
The parent's doors section ranks the $S$-unit machinery as the top door; it is
the right *shape* of door, and it is presently on the wrong side of the 1934
bound.

**(b) The route's own floor is below the target, so it is not self-defeating.**
Erdős–Stewart–Tijdeman construct $\alpha,\beta,S$ with
$\exp\big((4+o(1))\sqrt{s/\log s}\big)$ solutions, so $E(k)$ is genuinely at
least $\exp(c\sqrt{k/\log k})$ and no argument will ever make the Proposition
yield a polynomial bound. But $\exp(c\sqrt{k/\log k}) = \exp(o(k))$. **The known
extremal examples for the unit equation do not obstruct the bound Erdős #126
needs.** That is the reason to believe this door is open rather than walled.

So the narrowest upgrade, stated to be checkable:

> **Target.** Prove $E(k) = \exp(o(k))$: for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is
> $k_0$ such that for all $k \ge k_0$, all $k$-element prime sets $S$ and all
> nonzero rationals $\alpha,\beta$, the equation $\alpha X + \beta Y = 1$ has at
> most $e^{\varepsilon k}$ solutions in $S$-units.
>
> **Then** $g(k) \le 2 + e^{\varepsilon k}$ for large $k$, hence
> $\log g(k) = o(k)$, hence $f(n)/\log n \to \infty$: **Erdős #126.**

**Implication direction, explicitly.** Target $\Rightarrow$ #126. The converse
is false as an implication and should not be asserted: $g$ is a maximum over a
very special configuration (all of $A$'s pairwise sums smooth simultaneously),
while $E$ counts solutions of one equation with no such coupling, so #126 could
hold while $E(k)$ stays exponential. The Proposition is a sufficiency statement
only.

**Sub-target, if the full one is too strong.** The Proposition only ever needs
$\alpha = 1/d$, $\beta = -1/d$. That is, it only needs to count **pairs of
positive $S$-units differing by a fixed $d$**, i.e. $X - Y = d$ with
$X,Y$ $S$-smooth. A bound $\exp(o(k))$ on that one-parameter family, uniform in
$d$, is formally weaker than the full weighted equation and suffices verbatim.
This is the narrowest form we could find, and it is where a proof attempt should
start. Note it is *not* weaker than ESD's floor: ESD's construction is of
exactly this $X - Y = d$ shape, so $\exp(c\sqrt{k/\log k})$ is a lower bound
here too.

## 4. Additive energy, Freiman, sum–product: three tools, all inapplicable

The brief named these; the honest report is that none of them bites, and the
reasons are structural rather than technical.

**Freiman / small doubling: no hypothesis to feed it.** Freiman-type structure
theorems need $|A+A| \le K|A|$. Admissible sets have no such property: on all
seven of the parent's witnesses the off-diagonal sumset has size
$\binom{n}{2}$ or close to it, i.e. doubling is maximal, not small. Nothing in
#126 constrains $|A+A|$; it constrains *where* $A+A$ lives.

**Sum–product: the multiplicative structure is on the wrong set.**
Erdős–Szemerédi and its descendants lower-bound $\max(|A+A|, |A\cdot A|)$ by
exploiting that $A$ cannot be both additively and multiplicatively structured.
Here $A$ itself is unconstrained; it is $A+A$ that is multiplicatively
structured. There is no sum–product statement in the standard catalogue whose
hypothesis is "the sumset lies in a group of bounded rank", and inventing one is
the problem, not a tool for it.

**Additive energy: gives nothing beyond the trivial count.** Let
$A \subset [1,N]$ and $\Psi(x,p_k)$ count $S$-smooth integers $\le x$. Cauchy–
Schwarz on the off-diagonal sumset gives
$E(A) \ge (n^2-n)^2 / \Psi(2N,p_k)$, and $E(A) \le n^3$ always, so
$n \le 2\Psi(2N,p_k)$ up to the diagonal correction. But the *trivial*
observation that $a+b$ for fixed $a$ and varying $b$ are distinct $S$-smooth
numbers already gives $n - 1 \le \Psi(2N,p_k)$, which is stronger. **The energy
inequality is dominated by the one-line pigeonhole**, and both are $N$-dependent
and so cannot bound $g(k)$ at all. We report this because "use additive energy"
is a plausible-sounding instruction that measurably buys nothing here.

## 5. Audit of `r_186989` (Hunt #91)

Everything checkable was checked. Two corrections, one sharpening.

**Witnesses: all seven confirmed.** `probe.py` §A re-verifies every row of the
parent's table by independent trial division. All admissible, no violations.
Our own branch-and-bound solver, written from scratch, independently reproduces
$g_N(3) = 5$ with the same optimal witness $\{1,3,7,17,47\}$ at both $N=5000$
and $N=60\,000$. The parent's lower bounds stand.

**Sharpening of the parent's §4 (composition law).** The parent shows a
composition law $g(k_1+k_2) \ge g(k_1)g(k_2)$ would give $g(k) \ge 2^k$ and so
refute #126. That is correct, and it can be pushed to something falsifiable.
Fekete's lemma for the superadditive sequence $\log g$ gives
$\lim_k g(k)^{1/k} = \sup_k g(k)^{1/k} \ge g(1) = 2$. Erdős–Turán's
$g(k) < 3\cdot 2^{k-1}$ forces $\lim_k g(k)^{1/k} \le 2$. Hence the limit **and
the supremum** equal $2$, so $g(k)^{1/k} \le 2$ for every $k$, while
superadditivity gives $g(k) \ge g(1)^k = 2^k$. Therefore:

> A composition law does not merely refute #126. It forces
> $$g(k) = 2^k \quad\text{for every } k \ge 1 .$$

That is a sharp prediction and it is testable at $k=3$: it needs an
**8-element** admissible set for some 3-element $S$. Our exhaustive search finds
$5$ for $S=\{2,3,5\}$ inside $[1,60\,000]$. This is not a proof (the true
$g(3)$ could be attained outside the box) but it converts the parent's
"measured data is consistent with no such gadget" into a single named integer
whose determination would settle the composition law outright. **Proving
$g(3) < 8$ kills the composition arm permanently.** That is a finite,
self-contained sub-problem and the cheapest disposal of that branch we can see.
*(Landing note, 2026-10-21: Hunt #105 reached the same disposal from the
weaker corridor $[2^k, 1.5\cdot2^k]$; what this section adds is that the
corridor collapses to the single value $2^k$. Hunt #107 found no 6-element set
for $S=\{2,3,5\}$ with all pairwise sums $\le 10^5$, and Hunt #103 never saw
the first $k$ primes beaten over all subsets of the first nine primes for
$k \le 5$; both are bounded searches and neither proves $g(3) \le 7$.)*

**Correction: the parent's loose thread #3 is false.** The parent conjectures
that its parity cap generalises: "*the same argument mod small primes may cap
sets whose $S$ omits 3, mod 3 residue classes*", proposing $|A| \le p-1$ when
$p \notin S$. It does not generalise, and the reason is that $p=2$ is the only
prime for which every residue class is self-annihilating. For $p \notin S$ we
need $a + b \not\equiv 0 \pmod p$ for all distinct $a,b \in A$. For $p = 2$ the
classes are $0$ and $1$ with $0+0 \equiv 0$ and $1+1 \equiv 0$, so each class
holds at most one element and $|A| \le 2$. For any odd $p$, all of $A$ may sit
in a single class $r \ne 0$: sums are $\equiv 2r \not\equiv 0$, and the
pigeonhole is vacuous, giving no bound at all, let alone $p-1$.

Witness, found by exhaustive search and re-verified (`probe.py` §D):
$S = \{2,5,7,11\}$ omits $3$, and
$$A = \{1, 9, 13, 19, 31\}, \qquad |A| = 5 > 2,$$
with all ten off-diagonal sums $\{10,14,20,32,22,28,40,32,44,50\}$ free of the
prime $3$. So the mod-2 cap is a coincidence of $p=2$, not the head of a family.
*(Landing note, 2026-10-21: this thread was refuted on main before this arm
landed, in Hunts #93, #95, #101 and #103; the witness here is an independent
one.)*

**Consequence for the parent's doors table.** Its rank-1 door ("parity: $2 \in S$
is worth more than every other structural choice combined") survives as a
*measurement* but loses its proposed mechanism-as-family. The correct statement
is narrower: $2 \notin S$ caps $|A|$ at $2$; for every other prime, omission
costs a constant at most.

## 6. What we could not settle

- **No new upper bound on $g(k)$.** §2 hands the problem to $E(k)$; §3 shows the
  best available $E(k)$ is worse than 1934. This arm improves no constant.
- **Whether $E(k) = \exp(o(k))$.** That is the open problem, and it is open in
  the unit-equation literature independently of #126. We did not attempt it.
- **$g(3)$ exactly.** Our search is bounded, like the parent's. An unbounded
  argument that $g(3) \le 7$ is missing and would be worth having (§5).
- **Whether the coupling between the $\binom{n}{2}$ sums can be exploited.** The
  Proposition throws away everything except one pair $(a,b)$ and the two
  families $a+c$, $b+c$. All the other smooth sums are discarded. A reduction
  that uses more of them could in principle beat $E(k)$. We found none.

## Loose threads

  problem's real hypothesis (all sums smooth *at once*) is still unused, and any
  bound better than $E(k)$ must come from it. *First step:* for $n=4$, ask
  whether the four equations can be combined into a single Diophantine
  constraint on $(a,b,c,d)$ with a rank-independent solution count.
